## Key Takeaways

- The 22-point checklist we use covers spindle, ways, ballscrews, control, ATC, and hydraulics—each scored pass, flag, or fail before we advise on any used VMC purchase.
- Five red flags that kill a deal outright include spindle taper wear exceeding 0.0003 inch, coolant intrusion damage, ballscrew backlash above 0.001 inch, obsolete control boards, and hidden crash history.
- About 30-40% of used VMCs we evaluate carry at least one hidden repair bill above $8,000—catching these before signing is the whole point of inspection.
- A 2008-2015 era Mazak VTC-200 or Haas VF-4 in good shape can deliver 80% of new-machine capability at 35-45% of new cost, but only if inspection confirms it.
- Skipping inspection on a $45,000 used VMC to save $1,500 in eval costs is a choice we've watched shops regret within 90 days.

Buying used iron builds capacity without burning capital. The math works: a clean 2012 Doosan DNM 500 at $52,000 versus $140,000 new frees $88,000 for tooling or another machine. But the gap between a good used VMC and a money pit shows up at inspection. We've evaluated machines across Iowa, Illinois, Wisconsin, and Minnesota. The 22-point checklist below is what we walk through before giving a go or no-go.

## Seller claims versus mechanical evidence

Every listing says "excellent condition" or "low hours." Those phrases mean nothing without proof. Hour meters reset. Logs go missing. Crash history hides from spec sheets.

Mechanical evidence tells the truth. A spindle with 14,000 hours shows taper wear. A crashed machine carries signs in the column or saddle. Coolant intrusion in the way covers leaves corrosion that doesn't lie.

We run this inspection to read that evidence before money moves. In our log, about one in three used VMCs has a problem the seller missed or skipped. Not always malice—just the gap between "ran fine last week" and "will run fine next year."

The 22-point checklist closes that gap.

## The 22-point checklist: what we verify

We group the work into six areas. Each point gets a score: pass, flag, or fail.

### Spindle inspection (points 1-5)

1. **Taper condition** — We blue-check the taper with a ground test arbor. If spindle taper wear exceeds 0.0003 inch, we flag it. Above 0.0005 inch fails. Impact damage is a deal-breaker.

2. **Runout at nose** — We use a test bar at 1-inch and 6-inch stick-out. Under 0.0001 inch passes. 0.0001-0.0002 inch flags. Above 0.0002 inch means bearing wear.

3. **Bearing noise and heat** — We run the spindle at 1,000, 4,000, 8,000, and max RPM. We listen for growl. We check temp with a pyrometer. A 25°C rise after 15 minutes at 8,000 RPM is normal. Above 35°C suggests worn bearings.

4. **Drawbar function** — We cycle the drawbar 20 times. We check pull force if we can access the gauge port. Weak Belleville springs mean tools slip under heavy cuts.

5. **Spindle motor current** — At max speed with no load, we log current against spec. A motor drawing 15% high has drag, often from preload issues.

### Way system and geometry (points 6-10)

6. **Way cover condition** — We pull back covers and look for coolant intrusion in the way covers, chip packing, and torn seals. Damage underneath costs real money.

7. **Way surface wear** — On box-way machines, we check for scoring and galling. On linear-guide machines, we look at rail faces for pitting. New rails on a Mazak Nexus 510C run $4,000-6,000 per axis.

8. **Gib adjustment** — We check if gibs still have room. Maxed-out gibs with slop still present mean end-of-life on that surface.

9. **Geometric accuracy** — We use a level and indicator. We check table flatness, Z-to-XY square, and spindle-to-Z parallel. Good machines hold 0.0005-0.001 inch. Above 0.002 inch means wear or crash damage.

10. **Axis reversal error** — We run each axis through a reversal test. Lost motion at direction change shows backlash or servo tuning gaps.

### Ballscrews and axis drives (points 11-14)

11. **Ballscrew backlash** — We measure at three spots per axis with a tenths indicator. Ballscrew backlash above 0.001 inch on X or Y flags. Above 0.0015 inch fails.

12. **Support bearing condition** — We apply axial load while indicating. Worn thrust bearings let position drift under cut.

13. **Servo motor condition** — We check alarm history for encoder faults. We listen for motor bearing noise. We test brake function on Z.

14. **Axis tuning quality** — We run a circular test at 100 IPM on a 4-inch circle. Out-of-round patterns show mechanical or servo issues.

### Control and electrical (points 15-18)

15. **Control hardware age** — We ID the control and check parts supply. A Fanuc 16i-MB has a different future than a Fanuc 0i-MF. Boards for the 16/18 series are hard to find now.

16. **Parameter backup** — We confirm the seller has a full backup we can read. A control with no backup is a liability if the battery dies.

17. **Alarm history** — We pull logs and look for patterns. Repeated overload or servo faults tell a story.

18. **Cabinet condition** — We check for coolant entry, dust, bad wiring, and rodent damage. We've seen mouse nests in stored machines. This happens.

### Tool changer (points 19-20)

19. **ATC cycle test** — We run 50 tool changes in a row. Random picks. We watch for hesitation or miscounts. Worn cams and stretched chains show up here.

20. **Tool pocket condition** — We inspect for damage and wear. A machine that drops tools has damaged pockets. This causes tool-length drift and worse.

### Hydraulics and pneumatics (points 21-22)

21. **Hydraulic system** — We check level, fluid color (milky means water), pump noise, and pressure under load. We test drawbar, clamps, and any hydraulic ATC parts.

22. **Pneumatic system** — We look for leaks at cylinders, valves, and fittings. A leaky machine costs more to run and may have unreliable cylinder action.

That's the 22-point checklist. Every item ties to a machine that cost someone money.

## Five red flags that kill a deal

Some findings end talks right away. These aren't points to haggle—they're reasons to walk.

**1. Spindle taper damage or wear past 0.0003 inch** — A rebuild runs $6,000-18,000. If the taper shows impact marks or bell-mouthing, you're buying a spindle job, not a machine.

**2. Coolant intrusion damage to ways or screws** — Once coolant sits on precision surfaces, corrosion starts. Pitting hides until you wipe the surface. This signals neglect across other systems too.

**3. Ballscrew backlash above 0.001 inch** — New screws cost $3,000-8,000 per axis with install. Two bad axes mean $10,000-15,000 before you cut a chip.

**4. Obsolete control boards with no source** — Fanuc 16/18 power supplies and axis cards are gone from stock. A failure means a full retrofit at $35,000-65,000. We see this catch shops two years in.

**5. Hidden crash history in the geometry** — A machine crashed hard and not realigned carries errors that hurt part quality. When we see 0.003-inch square error on a 10-year-old box that should hold 0.001 inch, we ask questions. Bad answers mean we walk.

These five red flags that kill a deal come from jobs that cost more to fix than the machine was worth.

## What a clean inspection tells you

A machine that passes all 22 points isn't perfect. It's just known. You know the spindle has life. You know the ways work. You know the control has support. You know what's due, not overdue.

That lets you price right. A clean 2014 Haas VF-3 at $48,000 differs from the same box at $38,000 needing a spindle and screw. Both might be worth buying. Inspection tells you which price fits.

For shops thinking through [Buying & Owning Used CNC](/insights/buying-owning-used-cnc/) choices, the eval cost—$1,200-2,500 based on travel and machine type—is cheap next to a $15,000 surprise six months later.

## Cost of skipping inspection

We see this pattern often. A shop finds a deal on a 2010 Okuma MB-46VAE for $58,000. Seller seems honest. Machine ran last month. Price is right. Travel for inspection would add $1,800.

Six months later, the Z-axis screw fails. Replacement plus labor runs $7,200. Then the spindle starts making noise at 10,000 RPM. Bearing set and rebuild labor adds $9,500.

The $58,000 machine is now a $75,000 machine. The shop would have walked at $75,000. They didn't know what they were buying.

This happens most on private sales and auctions. Dealers sometimes have inspection reports, but verify the source. A report from the seller's preferred tech may skip things an independent check would catch.

The [repairs](/repairs/) we do often trace back to deferred problems that would have shown on a proper pre-buy eval.

## What an inspection costs

Time on-site runs 4-6 hours for a standard 40-taper VMC. Larger machines, pallet systems, or multi-axis setups take longer.

You get a written report with pass, flag, or fail on each of the 22 points. Flagged items include repair cost estimates. The report gives you data to negotiate or walk.
